Results

Predictive support differs among model components

The retained wheat-phenology formulation predicted 40,878 matched German station-test events with mean absolute error of 8.02 days, compared with 12.07 days for thermal time alone (Fig. 1a). The station split evaluates spatial transfer under observed weather and known sowing dates; calendar years were not held out. Support for the subsequently added upper-leaf stages and flowering-to-soft-dough window was more limited. The disease-field evaluation contained only five two-sided soft-dough observation intervals, with mean interval-excess error of 10.50 days (Supplementary Fig. S18; Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

Disease predictions did not improve consistently on calibration-selected benchmarks (Fig. 1b, c). Mean symptom-onset error was 5.32 days versus 2.79 days across 136 histories; the paired excess was +2.54 days (95% coordinate-year bootstrap interval 1.15–3.93). Severity RMSE was 31.19 versus 29.83 percentage points across 2,033 assessments from 188 field seasons. Model bias was -17.56 points, and the paired RMSE-difference interval included zero (-0.20–2.82). These retrospective comparisons exclude calibration records but retain uncertainty associated with prior model development (Supplementary Fig. S20; Supplementary Table S3).

Independent field environments also showed limited transfer of severity-based yield responses (Fig. 1d). Across five Nordic trials, endpoint severity gave held-out RMSE of 4.83 percentage points versus 5.02 for the training mean. Across two Tunisian years, the two-leaf model gave 3.32 t ha⁻¹ versus 3.24. Across five German locations, endpoint severity gave 18.51 percentage points versus 18.48 on 3,264 cultivar–management comparisons. Assessment-stage information improved Nordic error in a smaller subset, but uncertainty included no improvement and three of four fitted conditional slopes opposed a monotone damage response. German management covariates explained more predictive variation than endpoint severity. These datasets evaluate yield associations or management responses and do not validate physiological STB-attributable grain loss (Supplementary Figs. S22–S24; Supplementary Tables S17–S20). [14, 15, 16]

An additional audit identified 948 unique plot harvests, comprising 724 Swiss plots from ten site-years at five locations and 224 French plots from one site-year. In an exploratory Swiss location-held-out analysis, ordinal STB ratings gave yield RMSE of 0.926 t ha⁻¹ versus 1.142 for the training mean. Adding STB ratings to cultivar-composition and management predictors reduced RMSE from 0.914 to 0.877 t ha⁻¹, but five-site resampling showed unstable improvement. The French source lacked independent environmental replication. Neither source contained a compatible functional-canopy time series or an identified STB-specific yield counterfactual. These results extend statistical yield evidence without establishing end-to-end grain-loss validation (Fig. 1d; Supplementary Tables S24 and S25). [17, 18]

Published canopy–yield contrasts likewise provided partial support. Leaving each of five genetic backgrounds out of fitting gave relative-yield RMSE of 5.16 percentage points and R² of 0.247, but absolute-yield R² of −0.432 across eight treatment-mean contrasts. Only two contrasts involved STB alone. The linear conversion from normalized HAD loss to yield therefore remains a conditional index, retained in the supplementary evidence (Supplementary Figs. S9–S11 and S13; Supplementary Tables S5–S8). [2, 11]

Climate change redistributes canopy damage

Projected canopy damage increased on average while differing in sign among wheat-growing regions (Fig. 2). By 2071–2100, harvested-area-weighted normalized loss of healthy-area duration (HAD) changed by +1.45, +1.55 and +2.66 days under SSP1–2.6, SSP2–4.5 and SSP5–8.5, respectively, relative to 1991–2020. Mid-century changes were +1.11, +0.77 and +1.53 days. Normalized HAD loss is the time-integrated loss of assumed functional green leaf area divided by maximum reference upper-canopy LAI, expressed as equivalent days of canopy function lost during flowering to soft dough (Supplementary Fig. S25; Supplementary Table S9).

Under late-century SSP5–8.5, European normalized HAD loss increased from 6.30 to 8.95 days; climate-model changes spanned +1.23 to +4.57 days. The ensemble indicated increasing damage across 77.3% of available reference wheat area. All three models agreed on an increase across 65.2% and a decrease across 14.9%; signs differed across 20.0% (Fig. 2b; Supplementary Table S15). Model agreement measures consistency among climate projections, rather than predictive validation.

Area-weighted regional changes were +4.85 days in the Atlantic region, +3.45 days in the Continental region, +7.84 days in the Boreal region, -1.40 days in the Mediterranean region, and +0.37 days in the Steppic region. The largest mean increase therefore occurred in the Boreal region. Relative HAD loss increased from 18.03% to 26.07% across Europe, whereas symptom occurrence remained close to saturation (99.73% and 99.33%). Symptom presence alone consequently distinguished little of the projected change in accumulated canopy damage (Supplementary Figs. S14 and S27; Supplementary Tables S13 and S16).

Crop timing moderates weather-driven damage

First symptoms relative to flowering provide the primary timing reference because crop development determines the period in which damaged leaves support grain growth (Fig. 3a). The full-grid map describes this alignment; the sampled attribution distinguishes weather and phenology contributions to canopy damage.

On identical complete historical–future season pairs, symptoms advanced by 30.78 days after sowing, flowering by 27.32 days and soft dough by 27.07 days under late-century SSP5–8.5 (Supplementary Fig. S26a). Symptoms therefore advanced by 3.46 days relative to flowering. The elapsed flowering-to-soft-dough interval changed by +0.25 days. The much larger advance after sowing mostly accompanied earlier crop development, rather than an equally large shift in disease timing within grain filling (Supplementary Table S22).

In the separate 62-cell diagnostic sample, the weather contribution increased normalized HAD loss by 9.00 days, while wheat phenology reduced it by 6.36 days, giving a net increase of 2.64 days (Fig. 3b). The ratio of ensemble contributions corresponds to a 70.6% offset, with individual climate-model offsets of 63.6–80.3%. The calculation retains 5,731 valid season pairs and allocates the −2.78-day weather–phenology interaction equally between the two Shapley contributions. Its net estimate is close to the full-grid change, although the populations differ. This decomposition identifies opposing responses within the model; it does not estimate avoided yield loss or the effectiveness of changing sowing date or cultivar (Supplementary Fig. S16; Supplementary Tables S12a, b).

The phenology offset also differed among sampled regions (Fig. 3c). It was 39.1% in the Boreal region, 63.6% in the Continental region and 160.4% in the Mediterranean region. Values above 100% denote a phenology contribution larger than the positive weather contribution, producing a negative net change. These regional diagnostic samples contained 14, 18 and nine draws, respectively. Alpine and Black Sea regions had no draws, and Pannonian had one; regional sampling gaps prevent a complete attribution census (Source Data).

Production exposure changes regional priorities

The fixed SPAM2020 wheat-production baseline contained 241.84 million tonnes within the study domain (Fig. 4; Table 1). Under late-century SSP5–8.5, cells with increasing ensemble-mean damage represented 194.87 million tonnes (80.6% of baseline production). Cells with increases in all three climate models represented 162.21 million tonnes (67.1%). These amounts quantify the baseline production located in exposed cells; they are neither projected production nor tonnes lost.

The Continental region contained 106.62 million tonnes of baseline production, compared with 14.65 million tonnes in the Boreal region. Production in cells with unanimous projected increases was 82.81 and 14.17 million tonnes, respectively. The region with the largest damage increase per unit canopy thus differed from the region with the greatest production exposure. Surveillance and validation priorities depend on both impact intensity and the amount of production represented, while cells with differing model signs warrant observation of the direction of change (Fig. 4a, b).

Exposure did not increase monotonically under stronger forcing (Supplementary Table S21). Ensemble-increase exposure was 228.91 million tonnes under SSP1–2.6 and 194.87 million tonnes under SSP5–8.5. In contrast, unanimous-increase exposure rose from 97.04 to 162.21 million tonnes, while production-weighted HAD-loss change rose from 1.60 to 3.36 days. The geographic extent of positive mean responses, their intensity and agreement among climate models are therefore distinct indicators; a smaller exposed footprint does not imply less damage.

Production-weighted European HAD-loss change was +3.36 days, with climate-model values from +1.38 to +5.69 days. Fixed production weights describe the present geography of exposure under alternative climate pathways (Supplementary Table S21). They exclude future shifts in cropped area, yield, trade, irrigation and crop type. The imposed winter-wheat rainfed calendar applies to the all-wheat mask, so the estimates remain scenario-specific even where climate models agree. Country production exposure uses fractional source-country contributions within border cells (Supplementary Table S21; Source Data).

High-damage years became more frequent on an identical complete population representing 99.0% of baseline production (Fig. 4c). Under late-century SSP5–8.5, the fraction of years above each climate model’s historical 90 th percentile of European production-weighted canopy damage averaged 65.6%, with a model range of 40–90%. The three-model mean of the annual 90 th-percentile damage increased from 9.50 to 13.72 days. Exceedance frequency was non-monotonic across pathways. These empirical frequencies describe canopy damage on fixed cells, rather than calibrated probabilities of grain loss (Supplementary Fig. S29; Supplementary Table S27).

The European harvested-area mean was insensitive to 30 circular year pairings: the largest difference from the original late-century SSP5–8.5 estimate was below 0.001 day. Cells complete in every historical and future year and all three models retained 98.1% of baseline harvested area and gave a change of 2.68 days. Regional mean-change signs were unchanged across pairing and support comparisons. Reweighting by rainfed production gave a European change of 3.36 days versus 3.36 using all-wheat production. This tests aggregation coverage without resolving winter–spring crop representation or irrigation effects (Supplementary Fig. S28; Supplementary Tables S26 and S28).

All fourteen tested combinations of model structure, symptom detection and canopy assumptions gave increased HAD loss under late-century SSP5–8.5, with ensemble increases spanning 0.63–2.80 days. This more than fourfold variation in magnitude limits quantitative interpretation despite agreement in the direction of the European mean. Climate-model ranges, selected structural sensitivities and grouped field-evaluation intervals describe different uncertainties and cannot be combined into a single confidence interval for future grain loss (Supplementary Fig. S15; Supplementary Table S10).

Observed management responses remain environment-specific

The Nordic/Baltic 2012–2016 archive supplied 307 observed treated-minus-untreated yield contrasts across 263 reported trial identifiers and 25 country–year groups (Fig. 5a). The trial-weighted mean gain was 0.980 t ha⁻¹ (95% country–year cluster interval 0.804–1.142); nine contrasts were negative. Country–year-held-out RMSE was 0.628 t ha⁻¹ for the training mean, compared with 0.636 using treatment count and 0.638 using crop–treatment categories. Thus, the observed average response did not establish prediction of its environmental variation. Disease species, severity and functional canopy were unobserved, preventing STB-specific attribution (Supplementary Tables S29 and S30). [19, 20]

In the randomized Mauguio durum-wheat experiment, 195 mixtures with complete constituent references averaged 0.378 t ha⁻¹ above the constituent baseline, but 40.5% yielded less (Fig. 5b). The mean difference was 0.176 t ha⁻¹ using source spatially adjusted estimates. This single-environment comparison includes harvests without disease scores and overlaps the earlier French yield inventory. Shared controls connect 189 comparisons, precluding an independent-environment interval. Alternating-row experimental lines and fungicide protection after early disease assessment limit commercial and disease-mediated interpretation. In Switzerland, none of 637 eligible binary mixtures had both constituent controls within the mixture experiment; external national-network controls therefore supply noncausal diagnostics (Supplementary Tables S31 and S32).
