Results

Regional differences in projected Septoria damage

Projected Septoria-related canopy damage varied across European wheat-growing grids and emissions pathways (Fig. 1). By 2071–2100, the harvested-area-weighted normalized healthy-area-duration (HAD) loss changed by +1.45, +1.55 and +2.66 days under SSP1–2.6, SSP2–4.5 and SSP5–8.5, respectively, relative to 1991–2020. The corresponding mid-century changes were +1.11, +0.77 and +1.53 days. Normalized HAD loss is the loss of healthy leaf area over time divided by maximum reference upper-canopy leaf area index (LAI), with units of days. Larger values indicate greater accumulated damage. The averages combine areas of increasing and declining damage (Supplementary Table S9).

Under late-century SSP5–8.5, the European normalized HAD loss changed from 6.30 to 8.95 days; the three-climate-model change range was +1.23 to +4.57 days. The ensemble mean indicated increasing damage across 77.3% of the available reference wheat area. All three models agreed on greater damage across 65.2% of area and less damage across 14.9%; model predictions differed in sign across 20.0%. The continental increase thus coexisted with areas of declining damage (Supplementary Fig. S19; Supplementary Table S15).

Disease symptoms and canopy damage during grain filling

Symptom frequency changed little despite increasing canopy damage (Fig. 2). The area-weighted frequency of flag-leaf symptoms before soft dough was 99.731% in 1991–2020 and 99.332% under late-century SSP5–8.5, a change of -0.400 percentage points. Symptoms were predicted in almost every complete season in both periods. The mean fraction of reference HAD lost during grain filling increased from 18.03% to 26.07%, or +8.04 percentage points, with a climate-model range of +4.08 to +13.53 points. Relative HAD loss describes accumulated canopy-function loss; observed lesion percentage measures a different quantity (Supplementary Tables S9 and S13b).

Flag-leaf symptoms appeared 3.46 days earlier relative to flowering, with model-specific changes of -6.02 to -1.57 days. The interval from the fixed sowing date to first symptoms shortened by 30.78 days, spanning -45.39 to -18.74 days across climate models (Fig. 2b, d). Flag-leaf symptoms were present during 97.37% of grain-fill days in 1991–2020 and 97.42% in the future. The number of grain-fill days with symptoms changed by +0.24 days, alongside a +0.25-day change in the flowering-to-soft-dough interval. The large advance in symptoms after sowing accompanied earlier wheat development, with little change in the duration of grain filling. Timing estimates include symptoms detected in both paired seasons; frequency estimates also include complete seasons without symptoms (Supplementary Figs. S12 and S14).

Differences among environmental regions and countries

Environmental-region means differed substantially (Fig. 3a; Table 1). Under late-century SSP5–8.5, normalized HAD-loss changes were +4.85 days in the Atlantic region, +3.45 days in the Continental region, +7.84 days in the Boreal region, -1.40 days in the Mediterranean region, and +0.37 days in the Steppic region. Among the eight named environmental regions, the largest mean increase occurred in the Boreal region and the largest decrease in the Mediterranean region. The Boreal change spanned +5.06 to +12.52 days across climate models, compared with -1.88 to -1.13 days in the Mediterranean region. Alpine, Pannonian and Black Sea cells were included alongside the five larger regions (Supplementary Tables S13a–c and S16).

Country summaries also showed increasing and declining damage. Among the twelve country groups with the largest reference wheat area, late-century SSP5–8.5 HAD changes ranged from -2.02 days in Italy to +10.43 days in Lithuania. Yield changes inferred from their canopy damage differed in sign and magnitude (Fig. 3b). The full set of country means and climate-model ranges includes all country-assigned wheat cells within the study domain, with calendar eligibility and valid-season coverage reported for each group (Supplementary Table S14).

Yield changes inferred from canopy damage

Applying the reference HAD–yield coefficient converted the late-century SSP5–8.5 HAD change into an estimated disease-related yield change of -47.8 kg ha⁻¹ per unit reference upper-canopy LAI. The three-climate-model range was -82.3 to -22.2 kg ha⁻¹ per reference LAI. At coefficients of 0.0141–0.0207 t ha⁻¹ per green-leaf-area-index day (GLAI-day), the same canopy response produced a conversion range of -55.0 to -37.4 kg ha⁻¹ per reference LAI. A negative change indicates greater estimated disease-related yield loss. These estimates depend on the assumed canopy area and linear HAD–yield relationship (Fig. 3b; Supplementary Fig. S13).

Evaluation of the canopy–yield relationship showed limited prediction accuracy across genetic backgrounds. Eight treatment-mean contrasts across five genetic backgrounds gave leave-one-background-out relative-yield RMSE of 5.16 percentage points and R² of 0.247, but absolute-yield RMSE of 0.617 t ha⁻¹ and R² of −0.432. Two contrasts concerned STB alone; the other six involved yellow rust or mixed diseases. Models based on disease severity also had larger errors than their training-mean benchmarks. The grid estimates provide a disease-related yield index under specified assumptions. Total wheat yield and national production changes require additional crop-yield modelling and validation (Supplementary Figs. S9–S11; Supplementary Tables S5–S8). [2, 11]

Wheat development offsets part of the increase in disease damage

Changes in wheat development offset 70.6% of the increase in canopy damage attributable to weather under late-century SSP5–8.5 (Fig. 4). The weather contribution increased normalized HAD loss by 9.00 days, while wheat development reduced it by 6.36 days, giving a net increase of 2.64 days. All three climate models showed these opposing effects, with offsets of 63.6–80.3%. The decomposition uses 5,731 valid season pairs from 62 wheat-growing grid cells. Its net estimate is close to the full-grid increase of 2.66 days, although the two calculations use different sets of cells and valid seasons (Supplementary Tables S12a, b).

Flowering and soft dough advanced by 26.77 and 26.64 days after sowing, while mean grain-fill duration changed little. The wheat-development contribution combines changing leaf availability with changes in the seasonal weather encountered by those leaves. A −2.78-day weather–crop interaction was allocated equally between the two Shapley contributions. Twenty additional finite pairs were excluded because recombined weather and crop trajectories exceeded the accepted temperature range on an active day (Supplementary Fig. S16).

Sensitivity and prediction accuracy

All fourteen tested combinations of model structure, symptom detection and canopy assumptions gave earlier symptoms and increased HAD loss in all three climate models under late-century SSP5–8.5. Across these settings, mean symptom shifts spanned −5.46 to −1.54 days and HAD increases 0.63–2.80 days. These comparisons describe sensitivity to selected model assumptions and do not cover every source of uncertainty in future disease outbreaks (Supplementary Fig. S15; Supplementary Table S10).

Pooled field evaluation gave mean symptom-onset error outside the observation interval of 5.32 days versus 2.79 days for the phenology benchmark across 136 histories. The paired difference was +2.54 days (95% grouped-bootstrap interval 1.15–3.93). Across 2,033 disease assessments from 188 field seasons, severity RMSE was 31.19 versus 29.83 percentage points, with model bias -17.56 points. The paired RMSE difference was +1.36 points (95% interval -0.20–2.82). Wheat-stage predictions had station-held-out MAE of 8.02 days across 40,878 events. Disease prediction accuracy remains a major limitation on the interpretation of future damage magnitudes (Supplementary Figs. S18 and S20; Supplementary Tables S1–S4).
