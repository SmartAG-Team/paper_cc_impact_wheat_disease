Discussion

The simulations project increasing average Septoria damage across European wheat-growing areas, alongside decreases in some regions. These differences arise from weather effects on disease combined with changes in wheat phenology. Because symptoms were predicted in almost every season, symptom presence alone was a weak measure of the projected change. The timing of symptoms and the amount of healthy canopy lost during grain filling provided more information about the modelled disease impact.

The analysis links three processes: weather affects wheat phenology and disease progression; wheat phenology determines the leaves and seasonal conditions available to the disease; and disease damage reduces the assumed healthy canopy contributing to grain filling. The opposing weather and phenology contributions show why favourable infection conditions do not translate directly into proportional increases in canopy damage. The crop–disease interaction is represented through leaf availability and timing. Disease damage is converted to a yield estimate after simulation, while disease feedback on wheat phenology and a complete crop carbon balance are absent. These distinctions separate the projected disease component from the total effect of climate change on wheat production. [3, 4, 5]

The regional contrasts identify areas where crop and disease observations would be informative for adaptation trials. Greater projected damage in Boreal and Atlantic wheat-growing areas warrants testing disease resistance, crop calendars and canopy persistence together. Declining disease damage in the Mediterranean region also requires field verification because a decline in the disease component does not establish an increase in total grain yield. Country estimates describe the area included in the fixed wheat grid and imposed winter-wheat calendar. Differences in cultivar, irrigation, crop management and cross-border grid assignment limit their interpretation as national forecasts.

The link from disease damage to grain yield is the least directly validated part of the analysis. Disease-assessment percentages, functional canopy loss and grain yield describe different quantities. The assumed conversion from disease damage to functional loss requires physiological measurements, and the published HAD–yield coefficients were fitted to a wider growth-stage interval than the main flowering-to-soft-dough calculation. The available treatment means span mixed diseases and give poor absolute-yield predictions across genetic backgrounds. Canopy-based yield estimates remain conditional indices, with actual production losses requiring compatible measurements of leaf area, canopy function and grain yield. [2, 11, 12]

Disease-model performance adds substantial uncertainty. The model had greater symptom-onset error than the phenology benchmark and underestimated measured disease severity. High symptom-detection sensitivity with low specificity is consistent with the near-universal symptom occurrence in the simulations. Added developmental stages, including the end of the grain-filling interval, have limited informative field observations. Agreement among climate models cannot establish epidemic prediction accuracy. Reconstructed humidity, fixed inoculum assumptions, cultivar resistance and future pathogen adaptation also affect the applicability of the projected changes.

Regional adaptation assessments for European wheat need to consider disease resistance alongside crop phenology and canopy persistence. Coordinated observations of crop stages, disease symptoms, healthy leaf area and harvested grain would help identify whether adaptation reduces exposure, prevents damage or improves yield tolerance. Resistance and calendar trials across contrasting regions can evaluate these mechanisms under consistent measurement definitions. Intervention effectiveness and effects on production require validation in field trials. [14, 15]
