Wheat production under climate change depends on both crop performance and losses caused by disease. Septoria tritici blotch (STB), caused by Zymoseptoria tritici, damages wheat leaves and can reduce grain yield. Temperature, rainfall and humidity affect disease development, while changing climate also alters the crop that the pathogen infects. Understanding these linked effects is important for maintaining European wheat production. [1, 2, 3]

Weather favourable for infection does not by itself determine yield loss. Wheat development controls when leaves emerge and when grain filling occurs; disease development determines when those leaves become damaged. A shift in either process changes the amount of healthy canopy available during grain filling. Climate assessments based only on infection conditions can therefore miss changes in the timing of damage relative to crop development. [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]

For Septoria tritici blotch, symptoms follow a period of infection and disease development within the leaf. Rainfall, moisture conditions, inoculum availability and canopy development influence the seasonal epidemic. Upper-leaf appearance and unfolding are relevant because damage to these leaves can affect the canopy supporting grain filling. Healthy-area duration (HAD), the integral of healthy leaf area over time, provides a measure linking disease damage to crop function. Its relationship with grain yield varies among cultivars and depends on the leaves and growth stages included in the calculation. [2, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]

The unresolved question is how climate effects on wheat development modify Septoria damage across European growing regions. A linked wheat-development and disease model quantifies changes in symptoms and canopy damage across the complete wheat land-use grid under three emissions pathways. Environmental-region and country summaries describe where these changes occur. A separate analysis distinguishes weather effects on disease from the effects of changing wheat development. Published canopy–yield relationships provide conditional estimates of the disease-related component of yield change, while field observations establish the limits of model prediction accuracy.
